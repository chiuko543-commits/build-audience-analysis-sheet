# Audience analysis framework

## 1. Source synthesis

Before segmenting, summarize the campaign in a private working note:

| Dimension | Question |
| --- | --- |
| Problem | What is happening, to whom, and why does it matter now? |
| Solution | What activities, products, or services create the change? |
| Unique capability | Why can this organization deliver credibly? |
| Trust | What history, partners, results, or relationships reduce doubt? |
| Value | What changes for beneficiaries, supporters, and society? |
| Conversion | Donation, purchase, registration, lead, sharing, or partnership? |
| Constraints | Geography, timing, cultural boundaries, permissions, or missing data? |

Do not segment from the campaign title alone. A cultural festival, youth program, and corporate partnership proposal can share a brand but require different audiences.

## 2. Segmentation rules

### Core audiences

Choose three to five groups with a direct relationship or unusually strong relevance:

- existing donors, purchasers, members, subscribers, or followers;
- alumni, participants, volunteers, families, or staff networks;
- creators, instructors, partners, or their established audiences;
- local residents and venue communities when place is central.

Use core audiences for launch momentum, testimonials, sharing, and high-quality lookalike seeds.

### Opportunity audiences

Use the project's mechanisms to find adjacent groups:

- beneficiary need: parents, educators, professionals, advocates;
- activity: music, film, outdoor learning, craft, travel, technology;
- social value: education, sustainability, human rights, cultural heritage;
- supporter behavior: crowdfunding, nonprofit giving, ethical consumption;
- organization use case: ESG, employee engagement, brand partnership.

Write at the level at which creative and targeting can change. Split “education audiences” only when parents, teachers, and youth workers need different proof and channels.

### Resonance audiences

Use themes that can create emotional entry without pretending direct relevance:

- belonging, identity, family memory, intergenerational connection;
- local history, migration, return, land, language;
- self-exploration, creativity, care, community, or public participation.

Keep this tier smaller and test it after stronger opportunity audiences.

## 3. Row-writing rules

### Audience segment

Use a noun phrase that a campaign team can recognize and assign. Prefer `教師、社工與青少年工作者` over `關心下一代的人`.

### Psychology and behavior

Include at least two of these:

- current behavior or media habit;
- motivation or desired outcome;
- hesitation or trust barrier;
- decision evidence;
- relationship to the issue.

Avoid invented income, gender, age, religion, or education patterns unless supported by sources or required for lawful planning.

### Communication angle

Connect project mechanism to audience motivation. Explain why the issue matters to the group, why the project is credible, what one contribution enables, and what proof or story to show. Avoid repeating generic urgency in every row.

### Reach channels

Mix relevant channels:

- owned: email, LINE, website, social accounts, customer or donor lists;
- partner: creators, schools, venues, NGOs, professional groups;
- earned: media, podcasts, newsletters, community posts;
- paid: interest tests, geography, broad or algorithmic audiences, lookalikes, retargeting;
- creative: testimonial, short video, map, before-and-after story, process footage.

## 4. New-customer advertising guidance

### Interest tests

Create interest clusters rather than one enormous audience. A practical row lists five to eight candidate terms, for example:

- education: parenting education, youth development, experiential learning, school education;
- culture: cultural heritage, museums, oral history, traditional craft;
- music: world music, music education, instruments, live performance;
- public interest: nonprofit organizations, volunteering, human rights, social enterprise;
- place and travel: local culture, cultural tourism, ecotourism, outdoor activities.

Treat every term as a search hypothesis. Platform taxonomies vary by account, region, language, and time.

### Lookalikes

Prefer seed quality over size. Useful seeds include completed donations or purchases, completed registrations or attendance, repeated supporters, high-intent website events, and high-completion video viewers when conversion volume is limited. Do not combine unrelated low-intent engagers merely to increase volume.

### Retargeting

Separate warmer intent when data volume allows:

- viewed key campaign pages;
- watched substantial video portions;
- engaged with campaign posts;
- started but did not finish checkout;
- opened an eligible lead form or subscribed.

Match the message to the barrier: proof for skeptics, impact detail for evaluators, urgency for checkout starters.

### Sensitive identity and privacy

- Never infer or claim ethnicity, religion, health status, or another sensitive trait from behavior.
- Reach culturally relevant communities through contextual content, partnerships, opt-in lists, appropriate geography, and non-sensitive interests.
- Confirm legal rights and permissions before using customer or donor lists.
- Do not write copy that asserts a sensitive attribute about the viewer.

## 5. Quality checklist

- The project summary matches the strongest supplied source.
- The campaign conversion goal is explicit.
- Core audiences are high-intent, not broad demographic groups.
- Most rows serve acquisition when the user asks for new customers.
- Every row has a distinct message and reach plan.
- Interest clusters are short, testable, and labeled as suggestions.
- Lookalike and retargeting ideas name valid signals.
- No unsupported statistics or rewards were invented.
- The spreadsheet matches the reference layout or a compact five-column default.
- All rows fall inside the formatted range and render without clipping.
